          Methodology

          1 · Ingest

          Images are decoded (JPEG/PNG/HEIC/WebP), EXIF metadata is extracted — capture time, GPS position and optics. EXIF is the single strongest identification signal for solar-system objects: with a timestamp, planetary positions are computed to arcminute accuracy.

          2 · Measurement

          Sources are segmented above a robust 5σ noise threshold (median/MAD background). For the dominant source we measure: equivalent diameter, elongation and orientation from image moments, circularity, limb sharpness, interior texture, color ratios on unclipped pixels, chromatic-aberration displacement, and a light-concentration index — the physically meaningful discriminator between uniform disks (~0.11), centrally condensed galaxies (>0.22) and stellar point-spread cores (~0.3).

          3 · Astrometric identification

          If the frame contains a star field, its star pattern is blind-solved via astrometry.net (Lang et al. 2010) — geometric hashing of four-star asterisms with Bayesian verification, a method with a near-zero false-positive rate. EXIF focal length provides a field-of-view hint that accelerates solving. The solved position is cross-checked against SIMBAD (CDS Strasbourg), the offline OpenNGC catalog, and JPL-grade planetary ephemerides (astropy) at the EXIF capture time — so a bright disk at Jupiter's computed position is identified as Jupiter, not merely "a planet".

          4 · Probabilistic analysis

          When astrometry is impossible (no star field — the common case for zoomed or blurry shots), a rule-based hypothesis engine scores each object class against the measurements. Every claim cites its evidence; every report carries explicit caveats. Color-based composition inferences are labeled as analogies (Mars-like iron-oxide reds), never as facts: composition is a spectroscopic question, and the engine says so.
